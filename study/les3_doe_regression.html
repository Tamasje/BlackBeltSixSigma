Les 3 explained: Regression, ANOVA and Design of Experiments

A readable companion to Stijn De Vuyst's two slide decks, in the order they were taught: first "Linear Regression" (REG, 67 pages), then "1-way and 2-way ANOVA / Design of Experiments" (DOE, 94 pages). Grey page links open the slide itself (keep the source/ folder next to study/).

0. Start here

The one idea behind all of Les 3

Every method in this lesson does the same thing. It splits the total variation in your measurements into a part that a model explains and a part that is left over (noise), and then asks whether the explained part is large compared with the noise.

  • Regression (parts 1–2): the explanation is "the response follows a line (or plane) in x".
  • One-way ANOVA (part 3): the explanation is "the groups have different means". This is the same model as regression, with a categorical x.
  • Factorial designs, DOE (parts 4–6): the explanation is "these factors, and their interactions, shift the response". You also choose the experiment so that the effects can be separated.

The comparison is always an F-ratio: F = (explained variation per degree of freedom) / (noise per degree of freedom). If nothing is going on, F is about 1. A real effect makes F much larger than 1.

Two notations for the same critical value

The slides mix De Vuyst's own pages with scanned textbook pages (Montgomery), and they write critical values differently. This is a common source of confusion.

  • De Vuyst's slides use quantiles. t1−α/2, n−2 (REG p. 34) and F1−α, a−1, a(n−1) (DOE p. 7) are the values with probability 1−α/2 (or 1−α) below them. In his interval formulas the lower bound uses tα/2, n−2, which is a negative number: "β̂ + tα/2·se" is the same as "β̂ − t1−α/2·se".
  • The textbook pages use upper tails. t0.005, 18 = 2.88 (REG p. 32) and F0.01, 3, 20 = 4.94 (DOE p. 9) are the values with α/2 (or α) above them.

Both name the same critical value; only the subscript differs. In Excel: T.INV(1−α/2; df) and F.INV.RT(α; df1; df2).

1. Simple linear regression REG p. 1–45

1.1 What a regression model is REG p. 3–19

Y = f(x1, …, xp−1) + ε, ε ~ N(0, σ²): a deterministic part plus random noise
Multiple linear regression: Y = β0 + β1x1 + … + βp−1xp−1 + ε
Simple linear regression (one x): Y = β0 + β1x + ε, with β0 the intercept and β1 the slope

"Linear" means linear in the coefficients β, not necessarily in x. A model with x1x2 or x1² terms is still linear regression REG p. 6–7.

Always look at the data first REG p. 8–11: is the relation linear or curved, strong, weak or absent? And be careful: correlation is not causation. Ice-cream sales and customers' sweat production move together because a hidden factor, the day's temperature, drives both REG p. 11. A result must also have practical meaning.

Assumptions REG p. 16: at each x the response is normal around the line, µY|x = β0 + β1x, with the same variance σ² for every x (homoscedasticity; the opposite is heteroscedasticity). The true line µY|x and your fitted line Ŷ = β̂0 + β̂1x are two different lines: the fitted one is your estimate of the true one REG p. 19.

1.2 Least squares REG p. 17–18

Choose the line that makes the sum of squared vertical distances from the points to the line as small as possible. Setting the derivatives to zero gives the normal equations:

β̂1 = SxY / Sxx, β̂0 = Ȳ − β̂1 x̄
Sxx = Σ (xi − x̄)², SxY = Σ (xi − x̄)(Yi − Ȳ)

1.4 ANOVA and R² for regression REG p. 23–28

The "Analysis of Variance" table splits the variation of y into an explained and an unexplained part. Keep this idea in mind: ANOVA in part 3 is exactly the same split.

1.5 Tests and confidence intervals for β0 and β1 REG p. 29–34

se(β̂1) = √( MSE / Sxx ), se(β̂0) = √( MSE (1/n + x̄²/Sxx) )
t0 = (β̂ − reference value) / se(β̂) ~ tn−2 under H0
(1 − α) CI: β̂1 ± t1−α/2, n−2 · se(β̂1), and the same for β0 REG p. 34

Significance of regression REG p. 32–33: if β1 = 0, x tells you nothing and the best prediction is just Ȳ. Test H0: β1 = 0 in either of two equivalent ways:

  • the t-test on β̂1, or
  • the F-test F0 = MSR / MSE ~ F1, n−2.

For one x they always agree: F0 = t0². Remember the F-test: explained mean square divided by error mean square. It returns in every ANOVA table of parts 3 and 5.

Oxygen example
  • t0 = 14.947 / √(1.18 / 0.68088) = 11.35. With α = 0.01, t0.005, 18 = 2.88, and 11.35 > 2.88, so reject H0; P = 1.23 × 10−9 (p. 32). The intercept's t0 = 46.62.
  • Check: 11.35² ≈ 128.86 = F0 computed here.
  • 95 % CIs (computed here, t0.975, 18 = 2.101): β1 ∈ [12.18, 17.71] and β0 ∈ [70.94, 77.63].

1.6 Mean response versus a new observation REG p. 35–39

At a new x0 the fitted value is Ŷ0 = β̂0 + β̂1x0. There are two different questions, with two different intervals:

QuestionIntervalStandard error
Where is the average response µY|x0?confidence interval (CI)se(Ŷ0) = √( MSE (1/n + (x0 − x̄)²/Sxx) )
Where will one new measurement Y0 fall?prediction interval (PI)se(e0) = √( MSE (1 + 1/n + (x0 − x̄)²/Sxx) )
Interval = Ŷ0 ± t1−α/2, n−2 · (standard error)

The extra "1" is the noise ε0 of that single new measurement, so the PI is always wider. Both intervals are narrowest at x0 = x̄ and widen as you move away from it (pictures on p. 37 and p. 39).

Oxygen example at x0 = 1.00 % REG p. 22

Fit 89.231, SE Fit 0.354. 95 % CI (88.486, 89.975): the average purity at 1.00 % hydrocarbons. 95 % PI (86.830, 91.632): where one new batch will probably fall.

1.7 Checking the model REG p. 40–45

The assumptions are a linear relation, independent errors, and normal errors with the same variance. Check them with the residuals ei = yi − ŷi:

  • Residuals against fitted values (or against y or x): there should be no pattern, and the band should have the same width everywhere.
  • Normal probability plot: the points should lie close to a straight line.
  • Standardised residuals di = ei / √MSE: about 95 % should fall between −2 and +2. Points far outside are possible outliers.
  • Bad patterns REG p. 42:
    • a funnel (the spread grows): transform y (√y, ln y, 1/y);
    • a double bow;
    • a curve (non-linear): add or change x-terms.
  • R² with caution REG p. 44: adding terms always increases R², so a high R² alone does not prove the model is right.
  • Outliers REG p. 45:
    • An outlier near x̄ mostly lifts the line. In the slide's example the slope barely moves (17.5467 → 17.516), the intercept rises (2.6765 → 3.102), and the standardised residual is 3.62.
    • An outlier at either end of the x-range can tilt the line: an influential observation.

2. Multiple regression REG p. 46–66

Yi = β0 + β1x1i + β2x2i + … + βkxki + εi; k + 2 parameters to estimate (β0 … βk and σ) REG p. 46
Fitted plane (k = 2): Ŷ = b0 + b1X1 + b2X2 REG p. 48–49

Everything from part 1 carries over: least squares, the split SST = SSR + SSE, R², the F-test, and t-tests and CIs per coefficient. What is new is how to interpret the coefficients and how to choose the variables.

Meaning of a coefficient: bj is the change in Ŷ for one unit more of xj, with the other x's held constant REG p. 53.

Why not several simple regressions? REG p. 50 A simple-regression slope also absorbs the influence of every other variable that is correlated with X and Y (confounding). A multiple regression separates the variables as far as possible.

2.2 R² versus adjusted R² REG p. 54–56

Adding a variable always increases SSR and therefore R², even when the variable is useless. Adjusted R² penalises extra variables: it only rises when the added variable is significant enough. Use it to compare models; higher is better.

Which formula?

The slide on p. 56 prints R²adj = 1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − k − 2). The course's own outputs all use n − k − 1:

  • Excel for the oil data: 1 − (1 − 0.96561) · 14/12 = 0.95988, Excel's value. With /11 you would get 0.9562 computed here.
  • Minitab for the oxygen data (REG p. 22) and Design-Expert in part 5 (DOE p. 61).
  • The σ̂ formula on p. 57.

The calculator sheet shows both, labelled.

2.3 Polynomial models and the partial F-test REG p. 58–62

Polynomial terms (T², R², T×R) keep the model linear in the β's (1.1), so it is still fitted by ordinary least squares.

Worked example: acetylene yield

16 runs; Y = yield, T = temperature, R = ratio (data on p. 58).

  • First-order model T + R (p. 59): R² = 92.0 %, R²adj = 90.7 %, S = 3.624, SSE = 170.73 (13 df).
  • Second-order model with centred variables (T − 1212.5) and (R − 12.444), adding T×R, T² and R² (p. 60, 62): R² = 99.5 %, R²adj = 99.2 %, S = 1.066, SSE = 11.37 (10 df).

Are the three extra terms worth it? Use the partial F-test (p. 61). H0: all extra β's are 0. It compares how much the error sum of squares drops per extra term with the error mean square of the full model.

F0 = [ (SSE(reduced model) − SSE(full model)) / (k − r) ] / [ SSE(full model) / (n − p) ]

Here k − r = 3 extra terms and n − p = 16 − 6 = 10, which gives F0 = [(170.73 − 11.37)/3] / (11.37/10) = 46.7. That is far above F0.05, 3, 10 = 3.71, so keep the second-order terms computed here. Refitting the data here reproduces both printed SSE values.

3. One-way ANOVA DOE p. 2–20

3.1 From regression to ANOVA DOE p. 2, 5–7

Regression explains y with a numeric x. ANOVA explains y with a categorical x, called a factor. A factor is a variable with a few fixed values (its levels) that have no numerical meaning or order: for example material (aluminium, zinc, tungsten) or age class DOE p. 2.

The bridge

De Vuyst shows that the one-way ANOVA model is a special case of the multiple linear regression model of part 2. Give each level its own dummy variable and fit by least squares DOE p. 5. The fitted value for every measurement is then simply the mean of its group, and everything from parts 1–2 carries over DOE p. 6–7:

  • SSTotal = SSTreatment + SSError is the regression split SST = SSR + SSE. The slide literally writes SSTreatment = SSR.
  • The test is again explained mean square divided by error mean square, an F-ratio.
  • The residuals are checked exactly as in 1.7.

3.2 The question and the model DOE p. 2–4

The factor has a levels. Each level is a group (also called treatment or cell) holding n measurements (replicates). The question: do the group means really differ, or are the differences just noise? With two groups you would use a two-sample t-test. With more than two groups you use one-way ANOVA DOE p. 4. The course always assumes a balanced experiment: the same n in every group.

Two ways to write the same model DOE p. 3:

Means model: Yij = µi + εij
Effects model: Yij = µ + τi + εij, with Σ τi = 0
εij ~ N(0, σ²), independent; i = 1…a (group), j = 1…n (measurement)

τi is simply how far group i sits above or below the overall mean µ. The assumptions are: the errors are independent and normal, with the same variance σ² in every group (homoscedasticity again, as in 1.1).

H0: µ1 = µ2 = … = µa (equivalently: all τi = 0)
HA: at least one group mean differs DOE p. 7

3.3 Splitting the variation DOE p. 6

Write Ȳi· for the mean of group i and Ȳ·· for the grand mean of all a·n measurements. Compare each line with the regression table in 1.4.

3.4 The F-test DOE p. 7–8

As in 1.5, divide each SS by its df to get a mean square (MS), then compare the two:

MSTreatment = SSTreatment / (a − 1), MSError = SSError / (a(n − 1))
F0 = MSTreatment / MSError ~ Fa−1, a(n−1) if H0 is true
Reject H0 when F0 is larger than the critical value (α in the upper tail), or equivalently when the p-value < α.

Why F is about 1 when nothing is going on DOE p. 8. MSError always estimates σ². MSTreatment estimates σ² + n·Στi²/(a − 1). That equals σ² only when every τi is 0. So under H0 the numerator and denominator are "about equal", and a real effect pushes F above 1.

3.7 Checking the assumptions DOE p. 11, 18

The same residual checks as in 1.7. In ANOVA the fitted value of an observation is its group mean, so the residual is eij = yij − ȳi·. Check visually:

  • Normal probability plot of the residuals: the points should lie close to a straight line.
  • Residuals against factor level or group mean: the spread should be about the same everywhere, with no pattern.
  • If the spread grows with the mean, analyse a transformed response (log y or √y).

3.8 Which means differ? DOE p. 12

A significant F only says "not all means are equal". To see which ones differ, compare them graphically or use a formal method such as Fisher's LSD (named on the slide; its formula is not given). For the paper example: 20 % hardwood gives the highest strength, 5 % the lowest, and 10 % and 15 % do not differ from each other but are below 20 %.

4. Why plan experiments? DOE p. 21–45

So far the data were given. From here on you design the experiment yourself, so that the analysis of parts 1–3 can give clear answers.

Goals DOE p. 21–22: find which factors x influence the response y most; find the settings of x that give the desired y, the smallest variability in y, and the least sensitivity to factors z you cannot control; design new processes at minimum cost; improve the performance, reliability and robustness of existing processes; compare alternatives.

4.2 Factorial designs DOE p. 25–28, 41–44

In a (full) factorial design you test every combination of the chosen factor levels. The simplest is the 2k design: k factors, each at a low and a high level. Its advantages DOE p. 28: no confounding of factors, an unambiguous interpretation, interactions can be detected and sized, and maximum efficiency and precision.

The golf example DOE p. 27, 41–42

Two factors: driver type (O or R) and ball type (B or T), two rounds per combination. Scores: O/B 88, 90; R/B 93, 91; O/T 88, 91; R/T 92, 94. The square in the figure shows how effects are read:

  • Driver effect: average of the R side minus average of the O side = 92.5 − 89.25 = 3.25.
  • Ball effect: T side minus B side = 91.25 − 90.5 = 0.75.
  • Interaction: one diagonal minus the other = 91.0 − 90.75 = 0.25.

The three effect values are computed here; the slides show the figure only.

With three factors the square becomes a cube DOE p. 43. A fractional factorial tests only some of the corners DOE p. 44 (part 6).

4.3 Nuisance factors: blocking and randomisation DOE p. 29–33, 45

A nuisance factor affects the response but is of no interest to you: raw-material batch, operator, test equipment, shift, day of the week, weather DOE p. 45. It adds variation, so you want to neutralise it.

knownunknown
controllableblocking(blacked out on the slide)
uncontrollablerandomisation

In words: block on nuisance factors you know and can control; randomise against everything you cannot control, known or not.

Example DOE p. 30–33. Two factors A and B, 4 replications each, 16 runs in total. If the weather (C or c) might matter, make it a block: run every A/B combination equally often in each weather condition. Even better, also randomise the run order, so that unknown factors that drift over time spread evenly over all combinations.

Confounding DOE p. 45: two factors are confounded when the design cannot separate their effects. A good design keeps the factors you study from being confounded with each other or with nuisance factors. This is the experimental version of the confounding warning in regression (REG p. 11, 50).

Johan versus Nathalie: yield of a chemical reaction

The seven steps of an experiment DOE p. 39: problem statement; choice of factors, levels and ranges; choice of response; choice of design; running the experiment; statistical analysis (adapting the design if needed); conclusions and recommendations.

5. 2k factorial designs DOE p. 46–77

5.1 Coding the levels and the standard order DOE p. 46–47, 62

  • Every factor's low level becomes −1 and its high level +1 (e.g. 80 mm → −1, 130 mm → +1). All factors then have the same scale, and an interaction column (the product of factor columns) also contains only −1 and +1.
  • The design matrix can be stacked (one row per observation) or unstacked (one row per run, with the replicates side by side).
  • Standard order lists the runs so that A changes every run, B every 2 runs, C every 4 runs, and so on. In the labels, a lowercase letter means that factor is at +1, and (1) means all factors at −1: (1), a, b, ab, c, ac, bc, abc DOE p. 62.

5.2 What an effect is DOE p. 48–50

[Q] = (average response where Q = +1) − (average response where Q = −1)

This works for a factor and for an interaction alike. For an interaction such as AB, first make the column AB = A × B, then apply the same rule.

5.3 Interactions DOE p. 53–54

Two factors interact when the effect of one depends on the level of the other. In an interaction plot the lines are then not parallel.

Corners (A−B−, A+B−, A−B+, A+B+)ABABReading

The second row is the lesson. A's average effect is almost zero, yet A matters a lot, in opposite directions depending on B. Looking only at main effects would make you drop an important factor. (The slide on p. 53 prints AB = −1, but its own expression gives +1; see part 8.)

5.4 Contrasts and sums of squares DOE p. 55–58, 64–66

Let (1), a, b, ab also stand for the totals of the n observations in those runs. Multiply each total by its sign in the effect's column and add up: that sum is the contrast.

CA = a + ab − b − (1), CB = ab + b − a − (1), CAB = ab + (1) − a − b
Effect = Contrast / (n · 2k−1)
SSeffect = Contrast² / (n · 2k), each with 1 degree of freedom

For a 23, for example, A = [a + ab + ac + abc − b − c − bc − (1)] / (4n) DOE p. 64. The signs come from the sign table DOE p. 80: the column for an interaction is the product of its factors' columns. Each effect's SS is one line in the ANOVA table, exactly as SSTreatment was in part 3.

5.6 Which effects are real? DOE p. 67–71

There are two routes:

  1. ANOVA (as in part 3): each effect has 1 df, and F0 = SSeffect / MSE.
  2. Standard error of an effect, comparable to the coefficient standard errors in 1.5:
s.e.(effect) = √( σ̂² / (n · 2k−2) ), with σ̂² = MSE
effect ± 2 · s.e.(effect) is an approximate 95 % confidence interval; if it contains 0, the effect is not significant at α = 0.05 DOE p. 68

5.7 Only one replicate DOE p. 72–77

With many factors you often run each combination only once (n = 1). Then there are no replicates to estimate the error. The way out is the sparsity-of-effects principle: usually only a few effects matter, and higher-order interactions are negligible. So you pool the higher-order interactions and use them as the error estimate.

The full model with all interactions would use up every degree of freedom. This is called a saturated model; R then prints "NaN" instead of F-values DOE p. 76. It is the regression situation with as many parameters as data points: SSE = 0 with 0 df.

Worked example: plasma etch rate DOE p. 73–77

A single-replicate 24: A = gap, B = pressure, C = C2F6 flow, D = power; 16 runs (p. 74). The largest estimated effects (p. 75) are D = 306.125, AD = −153.625 and A = −101.625.

Pool the three- and four-factor interactions into the error (5 df) and fit main effects plus two-factor interactions (p. 77). Significant: A (F = 20.28, P = 0.0064), D (F = 183.99, P = 3.9 × 10−5) and A:D (F = 46.34, P = 0.0010). Everything else is not.

Conclusion: the gap, the power and their interaction drive the etch rate.

6. Fractional factorials DOE p. 78–93

Why DOE p. 78, 87: the number of runs grows as 2k. With k = 11 a full factorial needs 2048 runs, and even a half fraction needs 1024. A design for 11 factors in 32 runs is a 2−6 = 1/64 fraction. If you can assume that higher-order interactions are negligible (the sparsity-of-effects principle of 5.7), a fraction still estimates the main effects and low-order interactions.

6.1 The half fraction DOE p. 79–81

Take the full 23 sign table, including the identity column I (all +), and keep only the 4 runs where ABC = + (runs a, b, c, abc). In those runs the ABC column equals the I column, so the fraction is described by I = ABC. This is the defining relation, and ABC is the generator. The design is a 23−1: 3 factors in 22 = 4 runs.

6.2 Aliases: the price you pay DOE p. 81–83

To find what an effect is aliased with, multiply the defining relation by that effect, using the rule that a letter times itself gives I (A·A = I):

A · I = A · ABC = BC, B · ABC = AC, AB · ABC = C

In these 4 runs the column for A is identical to the column for BC. The number you compute for "A" is therefore A and BC mixed together, and the design cannot separate them. This is deliberate, controlled confounding (4.3); it is acceptable only if you may assume BC is negligible.

Projection DOE p. 83: if one or more factors turn out not to matter, drop them; the fraction then becomes a full factorial in the remaining factors. This is why fractions are so useful for screening.

6.3 Resolution DOE p. 84–86, 91

ResolutionMain effects are aliased with …Two-factor interactions are aliased with …
IIItwo-factor interactions (not with each other)main effects

The slides state the main-effect and two-factor-interaction parts of each row; the "three-" and "four-factor" entries follow from the word-length rule below.

Resolution = length of the shortest word in the defining relation DOE p. 91. Notation: 23−1III has I = ABC, a word of 3 letters.

6.4 Smaller fractions: 2k−p designs DOE p. 88–93

  1. Write a full factorial for the first k − p factors (the base factors).
  2. Make each of the remaining p columns as a product of base columns, using a design generator.
Worked example: 26−2 DOE p. 89–91
  1. Full 24 in A, B, C, D: 16 runs.
  2. Generators E = ABC and F = BCD give I = ABCE and I = BCDF.
  3. Multiply the generator words together as well: ABCE · BCDF = ADEF.
  4. Complete defining relation: I = ABCE = BCDF = ADEF. The shortest word has 4 letters, so the resolution is IV.

Choose the generators that give the highest resolution; a bad choice gives a lower one than necessary (p. 92). Montgomery's table on DOE p. 93 lists good generators.

The two questions in the DOE demo workbook
  • "2^5-1 fract rep", I = ABCDE: what is the resolution? The only word, ABCDE, has 5 letters, so resolution V. This matches the table on p. 93 (25−1V, E = ±ABCD).
  • "2^5-2 fract rep": choose generators for D and E with the best resolution. The table on p. 93 gives D = ±AB and E = ±AC (25−2III). The complete defining relation is then I = ABD = ACE = BCDE computed here, so resolution III. For example, A is aliased with BD, CE and ABCDE.
